A Unified Dynamical Systems Model for Deep Learning Loss

1. The Core Hypothesis: A 1D ODE for Loss

This section introduces the central goal: modeling the evolution of training loss $L(t)$ as a continuous trajectory using Gradient Flow. It establishes the basic ODE $\frac{dL}{dt} = -\|\nabla L\|^2$ and proposes the core hypothesis of projecting this onto a simpler 1D model governed by an effective eigenvalue $\lambda_{eff}(t)$ and a curvature function $F(L)^2$.

The starting point is the **Gradient Flow** Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):

$\frac{d\theta(t)}{dt} = -\nabla L(\theta(t))$

By the chain rule, the evolution of the loss $L$ itself is:

$\frac{dL}{dt} = \nabla L(\theta(t))^T \cdot \frac{d\theta(t)}{dt} = \nabla L^T \cdot (-\nabla L) = -\|\nabla L\|^2$

This equation is exact but not predictive. Our core hypothesis is to model this using a time-dependent scalar $\lambda_{eff}(t)$:

$\frac{dL}{dt} = - \frac{\lambda_{eff}(t)}{N} \cdot F(L)^2$

Where $N$ is the number of samples, $F(L)^2$ is a loss-specific "curvature function", and $\lambda_{eff}(t)$ is the "effective rate" of convergence.

2. The Framework: Defining Our Quantities

This section clarifies the two crucial, distinct definitions of the effective eigenvalue $\lambda_{eff}(t)$ that emerged during the research. Understanding the difference between the easily measured scalar and the theoretical Rayleigh scalar is key to the framework's validation.

1. The "Measured" Scalar (Fast & Practical)

Computed by inverting the ODE, this is a "mean-field" quantity relying only on the average loss $L$. It's the practical quantity measured in experiments.

$\lambda_{Measured}(t) \equiv \frac{N \cdot \|\nabla L\|^2}{F(L)^2}$

2. The "Rayleigh" Scalar (True & Theoretical)

Derived from first-principles (NTK theory), this is a "distributional" quantity depending on all per-sample residuals $r_i$.

$\lambda_{Rayleigh}(t) \equiv \frac{N^2 \cdot \|\nabla L\|^2}{\sum_i \|r_i\|^2}$

The calibration requires proving $\lambda_{Measured}$ is a faithful proxy for $\lambda_{Rayleigh}$.

3. Derivation & Analysis by Loss Function

Here, we analyze how the framework applies to specific loss functions. This reveals a critical difference: the mean-field approximation is exact for MSE but only an approximation for CCE, setting the stage for understanding the discrepancies observed later.

3.1. Case 1: Mean Squared Error (MSE) - The "Perfect" Model

For MSE, the analysis shows that the core identity $\sum_i \|r_i\|^2 = N \cdot F(L)^2$ holds exactly. This is because the relationship between per-sample loss and residual is linear.

  • Loss: $L = \frac{1}{N} \sum L_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum \frac{1}{2} \|r_i\|^2$
  • Curvature Function: $F(L)^2 = 2L$
  • Result: $\sum_i \|r_i\|^2 = 2NL$ and $N \cdot F(L)^2 = 2NL$.
$\sum_i \|r_i\|^2 = N \cdot F(L)^2 \quad (\text{Exact for MSE})$

Conclusion: For MSE, $\lambda_{Measured}(t) = \lambda_{Rayleigh}(t)$ by definition. The mean-field model is exact.

3.2. Case 2: Categorical Cross-Entropy (CCE) - The "Approximate" Model

For CCE, the relationship $\|r_i\|^2 \approx g(L_i) = \alpha (1 - e^{-L_i})^2$ is an approximation. Furthermore, the sum $\sum_i g(L_i)$ is not equal to the mean-field term $N \cdot g(L)$.

  • Loss: $L \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum L_i$
  • Residual: $r_i = \text{softmax}(z_i) - y_i$
  • Curvature Function: $F(L)^2 = g(L) = \alpha (1 - e^{-L})^2$
$\sum_i \|r_i\|^2 \approx \sum_i g(L_i) \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_i g(L_i) \neq N \cdot g(L)$

Conclusion: For CCE, the core identity is **not exact**. The mean-field model is an approximation.

4. The Central Scientific Claim & The "Tautology Trap"

A critical point in the research was identifying a logical fallacy. The initial validation, showing the ODE solution perfectly matched the observed loss when using $\lambda_{Measured}$, was revealed to be a tautology. This section explains why and refocuses the research onto the true non-trivial test.

The "perfect match" plot (like `image_4e5aeb.png`) seemed to validate the ODE:

$\frac{dL}{dt} = - \frac{\lambda_{Measured}}{N} F(L)^2$

However, substituting the definition of $\lambda_{Measured}$ reveals:

$\frac{dL}{dt} = - \left( \frac{N \cdot \|\nabla L\|^2}{F(L)^2} \right) \cdot \frac{F(L)^2}{N} = -\|\nabla L\|^2$

This is simply a restatement of the original gradient flow equation $\frac{dL}{dt} = -\|\nabla L\|^2$. The plot proved nothing about the predictive power concerning $\lambda_{eff}$ and $F(L)^2$.

The **real, non-trivial test** is comparing $\lambda_{Measured}$ to $\lambda_{Rayleigh}$, or more directly, testing the core CCE approximation:

Is $F(L)^2$ a faithful proxy for $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|r_i\|^2$?

5. Theoretical Explanation: The "Jensen Gap"

This section explains the observed discrepancy between $\lambda_{Measured}$ and $\lambda_{Rayleigh}$ for CCE. Using a Taylor expansion, it derives the "Jensen Gap" – the difference arising from approximating a sum over a non-linear function with the function of the average. This gap depends on the loss variance and convexity.

Experimental plots (like `image_4f9ebb.png`) showed $\lambda_{Measured}$ and $\lambda_{Rayleigh}$ have the same shape but are offset. The theoretical explanation uses a 2nd-order Taylor expansion of $g(L_i)$ around the mean loss $L$:

$\sum_i g(L_i) \approx \sum_i \left[ g(L) + g'(L)(L_i - L) + \frac{1}{2} g''(L)(L_i - L)^2 \right]$

This simplifies to connect the true curvature (related to $\lambda_{Rayleigh}$) and the fast approximation (related to $\lambda_{Measured}$):

$\underbrace{\sum_i g(L_i)}_{\text{True Curvature}} \approx \underbrace{N \cdot g(L)}_{\text{Fast Approx}} + \underbrace{\frac{N}{2} g''(L) \sigma_L^2}_{\text{The "Jensen Gap" / "Shift"}}$

The error in our approximation $F(L)^2 \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_i g(L_i)$ is the dynamic Jensen Gap term, governed by the loss variance ($\sigma_L^2$) and the loss convexity ($g''(L)$).

6. A New Hypothesis: A Phase Transition for Double Descent

Analyzing the Jensen Gap term revealed a potential mechanism related to double descent. The convexity $g''(L)$ of the CCE curvature function changes sign at $L = \ln(2)$, suggesting a phase transition in training dynamics driven by the loss variance $\sigma_L^2$.

The Jensen Gap term is $\frac{N}{2} g''(L) \sigma_L^2$. For CCE ($g(L) \approx \alpha(1 - e^{-L})^2$), the second derivative is:

$g''(L) \propto 4e^{-2L} - 2e^{-L}$

This term crosses zero at $L = \ln(2) \approx 0.693$. This implies two distinct regimes:

  • **Regime 1: High-Loss ($L > \ln(2)$):** $g''(L)$ is NEGATIVE (concave). The variance $\sigma_L^2$ term *reduces* the effective curvature, acting as a **brake** on convergence.
  • **Regime 2: Low-Loss ($L < \ln(2)$):** $g''(L)$ is POSITIVE (convex). The variance $\sigma_L^2$ term *increases* the effective curvature, acting as an **accelerant**.

Conjecture:

This phase transition in the *training loss* dynamics at $L_{train} \approx 0.693$ could be the underlying engine for the "peak" of the *test loss* in double descent. It may mark the boundary where dynamics switch from "feature learning" (braked by variance) to "interpolation" (accelerated by variance).
